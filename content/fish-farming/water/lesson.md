## wa-01: Why water comes first
sources: fao-hb-ch1

To raise fish you need a few basic things: land for a pond (or a suitable body of water), a reliable water supply, young fish to start with, and feed. You also need time, both to build a pond and to look after it regularly. Fish farming is real work, comparable to growing a crop like maize or rice. Of these basics, water is the one to check first, because without a dependable supply the rest cannot work.

## wa-02: Catfish farming in Nigeria today
sources: worldfish-2023-baseline

A 2023 WorldFish survey of 524 fish farmers and 66 hatchery operators in Delta and Ogun states found that catfish monoculture (raising only catfish) dominated, and that earthen ponds were the main type of facility. Farmers said the price of fish feed and access to credit were their biggest challenges. This tells you what is common among surveyed farmers; it does not tell you what will work at your own site.

## wa-03: Enough water, all year round
sources: fao-hb-ch2

A pond needs enough water all year: enough to fill it, and enough to top it up when the level drops. Rain alone is not a reliable way to fill a pond, because dry periods can leave the water too low for the fish. So the key question is not "is there water today?" but "will there still be enough water in the driest part of the year?"

## wa-04: Where pond water can come from
sources: fao-hb-ch2, lrrd-2022-water-sources

FAO's beginner handbook mentions springs, streams, lakes and reservoirs as pond water sources, and suggests the source should ideally sit higher than the pond so water can flow in by gravity. Water from streams, lakes or reservoirs can carry wild fish that eat your fish or compete with them; spring water near the site is usually free of them. In Nigeria, researchers have also compared rain, borehole, river and tap water for catfish breeding.

## wa-05: Not all water is the same
sources: lrrd-2022-water-sources

A 2022 study at the Federal University of Agriculture, Makurdi compared four water sources for hatching catfish eggs and keeping young fry. In that study, borehole water had the highest dissolved solids and hardness, and gave the lowest hatching rate (12.7%) and fry survival (55%). River water gave the best hatching (66.6%) and survival (98%), but the authors warn that river water can bring diseases from the wild. The study looked at eggs and fry only, not growing fish. The lesson for a beginner: water that looks fine can still differ in ways that matter, so it is worth having it tested before relying on it.

## wa-06: Signs of good and poor water
sources: fao-hb-ch2, fao-hb-ch11

Good pond water should not smell, taste or look bad, and should not be too muddy. Once fish are in a pond, warning signs of poor water include fish gathering at the surface to gulp air, eating poorly, or swimming weakly. Hot weather, overfeeding and too much fertilizer can all reduce the oxygen available to fish. One remedy in FAO's handbook is replacing part of the pond water with clean water, which again depends on having a reliable supply.

## wa-07: What "catfish can breathe air" does and does not mean
sources: belao-2011-air-breathing, fao-hb-ch11

African catfish have an extra breathing organ and can gulp air when oxygen in the water runs low. Research shows they do this as an optional strategy: they can live without surfacing when the water is well oxygenated, and breathe air more often as oxygen falls. This makes catfish hardy, but it does not remove the need for good water. Fish gasping at the surface is still treated as a warning sign in FAO's guidance.

## wa-08: Your location and season matter
sources: fao-hb-ch2, fao-hb-ch1

Conditions vary across Nigeria, and this lesson cannot tell you how long the dry season lasts or how reliable water is where you live. FAO's handbook advises avoiding low ground that floods in the rainy season, and checking that water will last through dry periods. A local extension agent or fish culture station, where one is available, can give guidance for your area.

## wa-09: Questions to answer before going further
sources: fao-hb-ch1, fao-hb-ch2, lrrd-2022-water-sources

Before thinking about ponds or fish, try to answer:
- What water source could I use, and is it close to where a pond could go?
- Does it still have enough water in the driest months, not just today?
- Could water flow in by gravity, or would I need to pump or carry it?
- Does the water smell, taste or look bad, or is it very muddy?
- Has the water ever been tested, and where could I get it tested?
- Could wild fish or disease come in with it?
- Who else depends on this water?

If you cannot answer some of these yet, that is normal. Writing down what you do not know is a useful first step.

## wa-10: Where to get help
sources: fao-hb-ch1, fao-hb-ch11

If there is a fish culture station or extension agent near you, they can guide you on questions this lesson cannot answer, such as local water conditions or testing. FAO's handbook also recommends contacting an extension agent or fish culture station if fish still show problems after basic care. Talking to an experienced local farmer is another practical next step.
