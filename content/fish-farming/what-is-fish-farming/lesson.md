## wf-01: What fish farming is
sources: fao-hb-ch1

Fish farming means raising fish, either to feed your own household or to sell for income. It is also called fish culture. A fish farmer looks after fish, usually in a pond, from young fish until harvest.

## wf-02: Why people raise fish
sources: fao-hb-ch1

FAO's beginner handbook gives several reasons people raise fish. It can make better use of land and water. It gives the family more food, and fish is a good source of protein. If there are more fish than the household needs, the extra can be sold at the market. More food and more money can help a family live better.

## wf-03: Catfish in Nigeria
sources: fao-2023-catfish-story, fao-fish4acp-nigeria

Nigeria is described by FAO as the world's largest producer of African catfish, and catfish is the most popular fish on the local market. FAO estimates there are around 285,000 catfish producers in Nigeria, about 60 percent of them small-scale farmers. Fish provides over 40 percent of the animal protein people eat in Nigeria, and at least a quarter of that fish is catfish. These are estimates, so treat them as a sense of scale rather than exact numbers.

## wf-04: How catfish farming has grown and changed
sources: fao-2023-catfish-story

According to FAO, catfish farming in Nigeria grew quickly after government hatcheries began supplying young fish (fingerlings) in the 1990s. Production then declined from 2016, mainly because feed prices rose. This shows that catfish farming is common and important, but also that it depends heavily on the cost of inputs like feed.

## wf-05: What you need to raise fish
sources: fao-hb-ch1

To raise fish you need land for a pond (or a suitable body of water), a reliable water supply, young fish to start with, and feed. You also need time, both to set things up and to look after the fish regularly. Fish farming is not easy: FAO compares the work to growing crops like maize, rice, cassava or groundnuts. You will also need to learn new skills, such as preparing a pond, stocking and feeding fish, and harvesting.

## wf-06: Common challenges farmers mention
sources: fao-2023-catfish-story, worldfish-2023-baseline

In a WorldFish survey of catfish farmers in Delta and Ogun states, farmers named the price of fish feed and access to credit as their biggest challenges, and said feed and young fish were their highest costs. FAO also points to the high cost of feed and limited access to capital as major constraints. The same survey found catfish farming can be profitable, but that depends on managing these costs.

## wf-07: Is it for me? Questions to think about
sources: fao-hb-ch1, worldfish-2023-baseline

Before going further, it helps to think honestly about your own situation:
- Why am I interested: food for my family, extra income, a business, or study?
- Do I have time to care for fish regularly, not just at the start?
- Could I get access to land or space, and to reliable water?
- How would I cover costs like feed and young fish until harvest?
- Who could teach me, or answer my questions?

There is no wrong answer. Deciding that fish farming is not for you, or not right now, is a perfectly good outcome of learning.

## wf-08: How to begin learning
sources: fao-hb-ch1

FAO advises beginners to learn step by step: how to choose a site, prepare a pond, choose and stock fish, feed them, look after the pond, and harvest. If there is a fish culture station or extension agent near you, they can guide you. In this learning path, the next topics look at the African catfish itself, then whether you have enough water, then setup options and the time and costs involved.
